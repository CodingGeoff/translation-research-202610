## 1. Opening: a survey with one item｜slides 3–5｜0:00–1:10

Today I am not asking you anything. But while I prepared, I wrote one item for myself, and I answered it.

**"After a few terms of interpreter training, your interpreting skill has clearly improved. How much do you agree?"**

My answer: strongly agree. Two hours of shadowing this week, and seventeen more note symbols than in September.

Now see the two diseases. First, **it pushes me to agree.** The improvement is already written inside the question. To disagree, I must argue with the person who wrote it. Second, **it uses a part to speak for the whole.** Two hours and seventeen symbols are parts. I used a part to score my whole ability.

And the hard part is this: **we cannot see interpreting skill.** We see notes, a recording, one grade. So today we follow one chain in two papers: how a concept becomes items, and how items become a conclusion. One line to keep: **reliability means steady, validity means correct.**

## 2. Good case: why this is hard to measure｜slides 6–8｜1:10–3:30

Zhou Jinhua and Dong Yanping, 2019, *Foreign Language Teaching and Research*. They wanted to measure **note-taking fluency** in consecutive interpreting. They closed four doors.

**Door one: the notes themselves.** Interpreting notes are **an encrypted file for the self of the next three minutes**. In class everything looks clear; three minutes later you must decode it. If a note survives three months, you still cannot read it — every mark depended on short-term memory, and that memory is gone.

**Door two: neat notes.** Since middle school we learned one rule: nice handwriting, good student. **Interpreting does not follow this rule.** A student whose notes look like a printed book may not tell you what the speaker said. Neat means "copied clearly." Fluent means hearing, recording, reading and using all run at once.

**Door three: speed and amount.** Letters per minute only prove fast hands. **Door four: other people cannot judge it.** Note-taking fluency is not visible from outside; a teacher sees the product, not the coordination.

So one door is left: **ask the learners.** And the words did not come from imagination. The authors **followed students over a long time** — recordings, notes, and above all their **reflections after every task**. Three sources: the literature, **72 learning journals**, and **interviews with 4 student interpreters and 6 teachers**. From this grew the construct: two stages, **four dimensions** — hearing–writing coordination, timeliness, system, use of notes.

## 3. Good case: 74 items to 28 to 21｜slides 9–12｜3:30–6:00

The item pool started at **74 items**. Five student interpreters and three teachers reviewed them. Then **three rounds of discussion, half a year: 74 down to 28**. Then a **pilot with 54 students: delete 7, keep 21**. Then the main study: **145 students, 144 valid**.

Why must 74 go? Not because it is long, but because **item forty is answered by the hand, not by the head**. A university screening with 100-plus items is a different thing: it wants big samples, not careful thinking. One design treats people as **data**, the other as **partners**. And if you drop 74 items into your class group, nobody finishes — not even with red packets.

Then look at **how** they cut. Not "too long, cut it." They ran **extreme-group comparison, item–total correlation, and homogeneity tests**; items that failed the numbers went out, and the paper shows why.

One more move I admire: the final scale was **renamed** "Self-Description of Interpreting Notes", not "Note-taking Fluency Scale". They were afraid we would read "how you see yourself" as "how good you are." **They fixed the name of the ruler first.**

**Steady?** Cronbach's alpha for the whole scale is **.901**, and every dimension is above .772. **Correct?** Three kinds of evidence: **content** — 8 experts rated every item, **CVI = 1**; **construct** — factor analysis found **four factors**, exactly the four planned dimensions, explaining **60.49%**; **criteria** — scores correlate with weekly practice (**r = .473**), with motivation (**.539**), and with interpreting performance (**.556**).

But the paper never says "practice makes you better." It writes **three "buts" about itself**: the sample is **homogeneous** — one university, one programme, one year, one teacher; **self-report is not the real skill**; the factor structure was tested on **one sample only**. **Good numbers are easy. Saying where your numbers stop is the hard part.**

## 4. Bad case: fair words first｜slides 13–14｜6:00–6:50

Yang and Mustafa, 2022, *Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies*. They asked what translation students think about **post-editing machine translation, MTPE**.

Let me be clear. **My criticism is not "this is a bad paper."** There is no principled error. It asks a real question, uses a mixed design, and codes the open answers in NVivo. Its one weakness is **the questionnaire design**. Small problems here grow big, because the sample, the alphas and the correlations all sit on those six scores. **A little blur in the items becomes a big jump in the claim.**

Design: students at **one university in western China**, **n = 127**; **six Likert items** (Q1–Q3 for perceived usefulness, Q4–Q6 for perceived ease of use) and four open questions. **Ten items in total. I will spend my time on the first six, because those six numbers carry the conclusion.**

## 5. Item by item: Q1 to Q6｜slide 15｜6:50–9:50

For each item I ask one thing: **what can this score say?**

**Q1. "Using MTPE would let me finish general and domain-specific tasks more quickly."**
**More quickly than what?** Faster than human translation, faster than raw machine output, or faster than yourself last term? These three answers point to three different teaching decisions, and the item picks none. Then **two task types in one answer**: if general texts get faster but legal texts do not, one tick cannot say so.

**Q2. "Using MTPE would improve the quality of my translation when translating into and out of Chinese."**
**Two directions in one question.** Students are usually stronger in one direction; one score mixes them. And **"quality" is never defined**: accuracy, fluency, or fitting the brief? A text can be smoother and less accurate — which box do you tick? Last, this measures **felt quality, not the quality of the translation**; the text was never scored.

**Q3. "MTPE would make me more effective and keep me competitive, so post-editing should be taught as an extension of translation competence."**
**Effectiveness, competitiveness, and a curriculum proposal — three things inside one score.** Say this fairly: **asking "do you agree" is not a mistake**; that is what a Likert scale is for. The mistake is that **one score cannot tell us which of the three a student agrees with.** He may accept that MTPE helps, and still oppose teaching it now. And in the end the paper **does not use Q3 for any teaching claim — it only enters the mean.** So why pack three things inside it?

**Q4. "Mastering the evaluation standard and the two levels of post-editing would be clear and understandable."**
**Two objects in one answer**: the ISO evaluation standard, and the full and light post-editing levels. If the score is low, you cannot tell which one is hard — so you do not know what to teach. Also, **"clear" and "understandable" overlap**: two words, one idea, half the information.

**Q5. "As a novice translator, doing MTPE regularly in future work would be easy for me."**
**This measures an expectation, not an experience.** Most students never took a real MTPE job; ask them about the future and they answer with imagination or confidence. It also stacks three frames — novice, regularly, future work — and "easy" is not defined. The cost: later the paper reads this item as "students are ready for the market."

**Q6. "It would be easy for me to become skillful at using MTPE in a CAT environment."**
Now the fair part: **I do not condemn this item.** "I think I can learn it" is a normal self-efficacy statement, and it fits the model. Two problems sit behind it. First, **feeling you can learn it is not wanting to use it, and it is not using it** — the chain is perception, intention, actual use, and this item stands at the first step. Second, the study **picked this one item alone** and correlated it with the Trados certificate score: **r = .746**. So a belief about learning became evidence about ability. **The item is not broken; the use is.**

## 6. Two breaks between items and conclusions｜slides 16–18｜9:50–11:55

**Break one: steady is reported, correct is not.** Alpha = **.714** and **.835** — that is internal consistency. There is no expert review, no pilot, no factor analysis. Sampling gets one line: "distributed online, 127 students", with no number sent, returned, or valid. So: **you can get answers without measuring the concept.**

**Break two: one item as a whole construct, and a claim bigger than the sample.** The study relates **Q6 alone** to the CAT grade and **Q2 alone** to the translation practice grade (**r = −.419**). But the same paper computed alpha **on three items per construct**: it knows a concept needs a set of items, then uses one. **That is like saying the whole team's strength is represented by player number six.** The right move is to average the items in a dimension, or model a latent variable, and then relate that to the grades. And a course grade is a course grade — same class, same term, same teacher — so the correlation may only mean "the students who did well there are more confident". Then, from "these 127 students are positive", the paper moves to "post-editing should be an important sub-competence in undergraduate translation programmes in China". **One university, two years, one region — a conclusion for the whole country.** There is no limitations section. A smaller sentence reads far stronger: **"In this sample, attitudes were generally positive; this supports a pilot in the curriculum."**

## 7. Four fixes, and the close｜slides 18–19｜11:55–12:40

Four fixes. **One: use a validated scale** — TAM already has Davis and Venkatesh items; if you build your own, say so, and run expert review, cognitive interviews and a pilot. **Two: one item, one thing** — one concept, one object, one time reference, and define the key word. **Three: combine before you analyse** — average the items in a dimension, and report sent, returned, valid. **Four: keep the claim inside the sample**, and write one honest limitations paragraph.

The gap between these two papers is not statistics. It is **restraint**. The good one renamed its ruler, so we would not read self-report as ability. The weak one used self-report as ability — and then rewrote the national curriculum with it.

Three lines for your screen:

**Define first, then write items.**
**One item, one thing; combine before you analyse.**
**The conclusion must not arrive before the evidence.**

Thank you.
