## 1. Opening: a one-item questionnaire｜slides 3–5｜0:00–1:58

Today I am not asking you anything. But I made a survey for myself, and I answered it. The item was this:

**"After a few terms of interpreter training, your interpreting skill has clearly improved. How much do you agree?"**

I answered: strongly agree. I can give three reasons. I did two hours of shadowing this week. My note symbols are seventeen more than in September. And I passed my last interpreting course.

Now look at this little survey. It has two diseases.

First, it **pushes you to agree**. The improvement is already written inside the question. If you disagree, you must first argue with the person who wrote it. So half of the "agree" answers are just politeness.

Second, it **uses a part to speak for the whole**. Two hours, seventeen symbols, one course grade — these are parts. I used a part to score my whole ability.

And here is the hard part. **We cannot see interpreting skill.** We can only see notes, recordings, and a grade. Using a part for the whole is not cheating. It is the natural thing our brain does. So the real question for research is: how do you measure something you cannot see?

Today we follow one chain of evidence in two papers: the research question → how a concept becomes items → who got the questionnaire → what the data can really support. And one line to keep: **reliability = steady; validity = correct.**

## 2. Good case: why note-taking skill is hard to measure｜slides 6–8｜1:58–4:33

Zhou Jinhua and Dong Yanping, 2019, *Foreign Language Teaching and Research*. They wanted to measure **note-taking fluency** in consecutive interpreting.

Why is it hard? They closed four doors.

**Door one: the notes themselves.** Student notes are **an encrypted file for the self of the next three minutes**. In class, everything looks clear. Three minutes later you must decode it first. If a note survives three months, you still cannot read it — because every mark depended on short-term memory, and that memory is gone.

**Door two: neat notes.** In school we learned a simple rule: nice handwriting, good student. **Interpreting does not follow this rule.** A student whose notes look like a printed book may not be able to tell you what the speaker said. Neat means "copied clearly." Fluent means "hearing, recording, reading and using all run at the same time."

**Door three: speed and amount.** Letters per minute, or the gap between hearing and writing — these work for handwriting studies. For interpreting notes they only prove fast hands.

**Door four: other people cannot judge it.** Language fluency is heard. Note-taking fluency is not visible from outside. A teacher can see the product, not the coordination.

So one door is left: **ask the learners themselves.** This is not a weak choice. Learners feel the stuck point first.

**Where did the language come from?** Not from the researchers' imagination. They **watched and followed students over time**: recordings, notes, and above all the students' own **reflections after each interpreting task**. They collected **72 learning journals**, plus **interviews with 4 student interpreters and 6 interpreting teachers**. From this came the construct: two stages (recording and reading), **four dimensions** — hearing–writing coordination, timeliness, system, and use of notes.

One side note on method: quantitative work in our field does not appear from nowhere. It grows from **many small, plain observations**. Quantity first, numbers later.

## 3. Good case: 74 items → 28 → 21, with reasons on paper｜slides 9–12｜4:33–6:56

The item pool started at **74 items**. Five student interpreters and three teachers reviewed them. Then **three rounds of discussion, half a year: 74 → 28**. Then a **pilot with 54 students: delete 7, keep 21**. Then the main study: **145 students answered, 144 valid**.

Why must 74 go? Not because it is tiring — because it is **unfair to the respondent**. Item forty will be answered by the hand, not by the head. A university psychological screening with 100+ items is different: it wants big samples, not careful thinking. There are two values here: one treats people as **data**, one as **partners**.

Then look at **how** items were deleted. This is the real lesson. Deleting items is not "too long, cut it." It is **extreme-group comparison, item–total correlation, homogeneity testing**. Items that fail, go; and the paper shows why.

One more move I really like: the final scale is **renamed** "Self-Description of Interpreting Notes." Not "Note-taking Fluency Scale." They were afraid we would read "how you see yourself" as "how good you are." **They fixed the name of the ruler first.** Many papers forget this: the items measure self-report, but the conclusion talks about ability.

**Steady?** Cronbach's alpha for the whole scale is **.901**; the four dimensions are .812, .772, .803, .859. Ten of the 21 items are **reverse-scored**, so nobody can slide down one column.

**Correct?** Three kinds of evidence:
- **Content validity**: 8 experts rated every item; **CVI = 1**.
- **Construct validity**: factor analysis found **four factors** — exactly the four planned dimensions, explaining **60.49%** of the variance. The map in the authors' head and the map in the data matched.
- **Criterion validity**: scores correlate with practice time (**r = .473**, 22.4% of variance), with motivation (**r = .539**), and with interpreting performance (**r = .556**, 30.9%).

And then the paper writes **three "buts"** about itself: the sample is **homogeneous** (one university, one programme, one year, one teacher); **self-report is not the real skill**; the factor structure was tested on **one sample only**. It never says "more practice makes you better." It says "practice time predicts the score."

**Good numbers are easy. Saying where your numbers stop is the hard part.**

## 4. Bad case: first, what it does well｜slides 13–14｜6:56–8:21

Yang and Mustafa, 2022, *Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies*. They asked: what do translation students think about **post-editing machine translation (MTPE)**?

Let me be fair, because this matters. **My criticism is not "this is a bad paper."** There is no principled error. It asks a real question. It uses a mixed design: SPSS for the numbers, NVivo coding for the open answers. It removed 18 low-relevance answers on its own. It reports reliability **by construct**, not one mixed alpha.

The one weak place is **the questionnaire design**. Small problems here grow big later, because every sample, every alpha, every correlation sits on those item scores. **A little blur in the items becomes a big jump in the claim.**

Design: students of translation at **one university in western China**, **n = 127**. **Six Likert items** (Q1–Q3 for perceived usefulness, Q4–Q6 for perceived ease of use) plus **four open questions** (Q7–Q10). **Ten items in total. I will spend most of my time on the first six, because those six numbers carry the conclusion.**

## 5. Item by item: Q1 to Q6｜slide 15｜8:21–13:58

For each item I ask one thing only: **what can this score say?**

**Q1. "Using MTPE would let me finish general and domain-specific tasks more quickly."**
Two problems. First, **more quickly than what?** Faster than human translation? Faster than raw machine output? Faster than last term? These three answers lead to three different teaching decisions, and the item does not choose. Second, **two task types in one answer**. If general texts get faster but legal texts do not, one tick cannot say so. Note also: "more quickly" is carried over from the original TAM wording. The wording was borrowed; the reference point was not fixed.

**Q2. "Using MTPE would improve the quality of my translation when translating into and out of Chinese."**
First, **two directions in one question**. Students are usually stronger in one direction; one score mixes them. Second, **"quality" is not defined**: accuracy, fluency, or fitting the brief? A text can be smoother and less accurate — which box do you tick then? Third, this item measures **felt quality, not actual quality of the译文** — the target text was never scored.

**Q3. "MTPE would make me more effective and keep me competitive, so post-editing should be taught as an extension of translation competence."**
Say this slowly: **effectiveness, competitiveness, and a curriculum proposal — three things inside one score.** Asking "do you agree?" is not a mistake; that is what a Likert scale is for. The mistake is that **one score cannot tell us which of the three a student agrees with**. He may accept that MTPE helps, but oppose teaching it now. And after all the data: **Q3 is not used for any teaching claim — it only enters the mean**. So why pack three things inside it?

**Q4. "Mastering the evaluation standard and the two levels of post-editing would be clear and understandable."**
**Two objects, one answer**: the ISO evaluation standard, and the full / light post-editing levels. If the score is low, we do not know which one is hard — so we do not know what to teach. Also, **"clear" and "understandable" overlap**; two words, one idea, half the information. By the way, this item has the largest spread of the six (SD ≈ 0.94) — students really did disagree. The item just cannot tell us where.

**Q5. "As a novice translator, doing MTPE regularly in future work would be easy for me."**
**This measures an expectation, not an experience.** Most students have never taken a real MTPE job. Ask them about the future and they answer with imagination or confidence. The item also stacks three frames at once: identity (novice), frequency (regularly), setting (future work). And "easy" is not defined — low difficulty, or "I can take it and keep the volume"? One unclear word, and later the paper reads this item as "students are ready for the market."

**Q6. "It would be easy for me to become skillful at using MTPE in a CAT environment."**
Now the honest part: **this item is fine. I do not condemn it.** "I think I can learn it" is a normal self-efficacy item, and it fits TAM.
The problem is what happens next. **Feeling you can learn it is not the same as wanting to use it, and not the same as using it.** In Davis's model the chain runs: perception → intention → actual use. This item stands at the first step only. And the study **picked this one item alone** and correlated it with the Trados certificate score (**r = .746**). So a self-report about learning became a measure of technical competence. **The item is not broken; the use is.**

The four open items repeat the same habits: **Q8 asks two things at once** ("what do you enjoy most" and "what are the obstacles"); **Q9 asks three** (continue? increase? across five text types? explain); **Q10 mixes a yes/no question, a market fact, and "are you aware"** — which quietly hints that the standard is right and you should agree. So this is not bad luck on one item. **The same habit runs through all ten.**

## 6. From items to conclusions: three breaks｜slides 16–18｜13:58–17:01

**Break one: sample and procedure.** One line tells us everything: "distributed online, 127 students." No number sent, no number returned, no number valid, no statement on whether whole classes or volunteers answered. Volunteers are already more positive — that changes the direction, not only the size.

**Break two: steady is reported, correct is not.** Alpha = **.714** and **.835**. Those are internal consistency. There is **no** expert review, **no** pilot, **no** factor analysis. So: **you can get answers without measuring the concept.** And one more: the 6-point scale has **no neutral point**, yet the paper writes "all means are above the midpoint, so attitudes are positive." On a scale without a middle, "not negative" is not "positive." Ordinal numbers are being read as interval numbers.

**Break three: one item as a whole construct.** The study correlates **Q6 with the CAT course grade (r = .746)** and **Q2 with the translation practice grade (r = −.419)** — one item at a time. But the same paper computed alpha **on three items per construct**. It knows a concept needs a set of items; then, in the correlation, it uses one. That is like saying: the whole team's strength is represented by player number six. The right move: average the items in the same dimension, or model a latent variable, and then relate that to the grades.
Also: a **course grade is a course grade** — same course, same term, same teacher. A correlation with it may just mean "students who did well in that class are more confident." And the juniors finished the CAT course last term, the seniors one year earlier; the paper compares their means (Q3: 4.13 vs 4.56; Q6: 3.90 vs 4.74) but **never treats year or course timing as a confound**. Then, of course, **correlation is not causation**. This is not a long step — it is a jump.

**And the last break: the range of the claim.** From "these 127 students are positive" to "post-editing should be an important sub-competence in undergraduate translation programmes in China." One university, two years, one region — and a conclusion for the whole country. There is no limitations section. A safer sentence costs nothing: **"In this sample, students' attitudes were generally positive; this supports a pilot in the curriculum."** Same evidence, ten times the credibility.

## 7. Five fixes｜slide 18｜17:01–17:48

1. **Use a validated scale** — TAM already has Davis / Venkatesh items. Do not shorten them by hand and call it a scale.
2. **If you build items, say so and test them**: source, expert review (CVI), **cognitive interviews** ("what are you thinking when you read this item?"), a pilot with item analysis.
3. **One item, one thing**: one concept, one object, one time reference; define the key word inside the item.
4. **Combine before you analyse**: average items in the same dimension; report sent / returned / valid.
5. **Keep the claim inside the sample**, and write one short limitations paragraph.

## 8. Closing｜slide 19｜17:48–18:27

The gap between these two papers may not be statistics. It is **restraint**. The good one renamed its scale, so we would not read self-report as ability. The weak one used self-report as ability — and then rewrote the national curriculum with it.

Three lines for the sticky note on your screen:

**Define first, then write items.**
**One item, one thing; combine items before you analyse.**
**The conclusion must not arrive before the evidence.**

Thank you.
