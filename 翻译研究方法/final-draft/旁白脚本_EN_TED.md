# English narration (TED style)｜One careful survey, one careless one

> **Use**: a short English companion to the Chinese voice-over. Same two cases: Zhou & Dong's interpreting note-taking scale (good), and an MTPE attitude questionnaire (weak).
> **Style**: short sentences, one idea each, plain words. Condensed and adapted — not a word-for-word translation.
> **Length**: about 620 words, roughly 4–5 minutes at a calm TED pace.
> **Note**: page markers and slide cues are removed; there is nothing to click, nothing to read off the screen.

## Narration

### Hook

Can you tell how good an interpreter is, just by looking at their notes?

Neither can I. And that is exactly the problem.

Today I want to show you two questionnaires. One was built slowly. One was built fast. The difference between them is the difference between knowing what you measured — and guessing.

### The good case: measuring something you cannot see

Interpreting notes are an encrypted file, written for the self of the next three minutes. Right after the meeting, you can still read them. Three months later, they are noise.

And here is the trap. Neat notes do not mean good interpreting. In middle school we learned one rule: nice handwriting, good student. Interpreting does not follow that rule. A beautiful page of notes can hide a broken message.

So how do you measure something you cannot see?

Zhou Jinhua and Dong Yanping, two Chinese researchers, tried something simple and honest. They collected students' learning journals, students' self-reports, and teacher interviews. From all of that, they wrote seventy-four items.

Seventy-four questions. Now imagine asking a stranger to answer all of them. This is not a school test; nobody has to fill it in. Even a red packet will not buy that much patience.

So the team reviewed, piloted, cut, and cut again. They kept twenty-one items.

Then they tested their scale. Reliability — how steady it is — reached a Cronbach alpha of 0.901. Validity — how correct it is — was checked three ways: experts for content, factor analysis for structure, and real interpreting scores for criterion.

And then they did the bravest thing in research. They wrote down their own limits. Self-report is not real skill. One sample is one sample. Correlation is not cause.

### The weak case: a survey that outran its evidence

Now the second study. A survey of translation undergraduates, asking how they feel about machine translation post-editing — MTPE, for short.

The question is worth asking. The design is thin. Six Likert items, four open questions. It borrows a well-known framework, the Technology Acceptance Model, but it does not borrow a validated scale. Every item was written by hand. No expert review. No pilot test.

Let me show you what that costs, one item at a time.

Item one: post-editing helps you finish ordinary and specialized tasks faster. Faster than what? Than translating alone? Than raw machine output? Everyone compares against something different, so every score means something different. And two different tasks got squeezed into one question.

Item two: does post-editing improve quality in both directions? Two directions, one score. And what is quality? Accuracy? Fluency? Client satisfaction? We will never know.

Item three: one item, three ideas — efficiency, competitiveness, and adding post-editing to the curriculum. One score cannot hold three opinions.

Item four: are the evaluation criteria and the two levels of post-editing clear and easy to understand? Two concepts, one answer. And "clear" and "easy to understand" mean the same thing anyway.

Item five: will post-editing be easy in your future job? These students have not had that job. They are guessing.

Item six: can you easily learn post-editing skills? This one is not broken. But it measures a feeling, not a behaviour. Here is the maths-class version. Everyone nods at the shortcut method in class; almost nobody dares to use it in the exam. Understanding is not willingness. Willingness is not skill.

### The analysis, and the leap

Then comes the analysis. The authors correlated single items with course grades. In survey research, items in one dimension are meant to be combined into a score before you correlate. One item is a shaky ruler. And even a strong correlation is not a cause.

Then comes the conclusion. One university. A few dozen translation majors. And a claim about all translation students in the country.

### Close

So, two studies, three things to take home.

Before you write the items: define the thing you are measuring.
Before you trust the survey: test it, then test it again.
And before you write the conclusion: ask how far your evidence can really travel.

Thank you.
